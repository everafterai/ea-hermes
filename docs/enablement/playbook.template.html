Base · Off Your Plate

Three hours to turn twenty people into automation owners

A working session on what our automations can do, what they are deliberately not allowed to do, and what each team is going to build on the day.

3 hours ~20 people 6 squads 1 champion each
The point

What has to be true when everyone walks out

The failure mode for a session like this is a room full of polite nodding and zero adoption. People find the capabilities interesting, nobody maps them onto their own Tuesday, and in two weeks nothing has changed. So the session is built backwards from four outcomes.

  • Everyone can describe an automation in one sentence. Trigger, context, judgment, action — the same four blocks every time.
  • Every squad has designed one automation written on a worksheet, including the access it needs.
  • Every squad has a named champion with a role, a channel, and a scope card.
  • Every squad demos something running before anyone leaves the room.

That last one is the accountability loop, and it is why the demos sit inside the session rather than in a follow-up meeting two weeks later. A list of ideas decays inside a week; a thing that ran in front of colleagues does not.

Prep

Before the day

The week before

  • Pick the six champions — curiosity over seniority
  • Create a Slack channel per squad
  • Assign champion roles in config.yaml so nobody is blocked on permissions during the build block
  • Write six scope cards — one per squad
  • Point the automation-creation monitor and the weekly activity report at a channel the whole company can see — that is the ongoing loop, in place of a follow-up meeting

The day before

  • Create #radar
  • Assemble the watch list: the customer logos already public on base.ai, plus target prospects and two or three competitors
  • Rehearse the exact demo build, end to end, then tear it down
  • Note which item in the output is the one to point at — ideally about an account someone in the room owns
  • Have a screen recording of the successful run as a fallback
Why rehearsal matters more than usual here. The demo's payoff depends on the agent surfacing something genuinely surprising. Running it the day before tells you whether the news is rich or thin — and if it's thin, you widen the list before the room is watching rather than after.

Teaching block · the core idea

Every automation is four blocks

This is the one thing everybody has to leave knowing. Teach it once, then use the same four words for the rest of the session — in the demo, on the worksheet, in the share-back.

Trigger

What sets it off. Someone posts in a channel, mentions it, or a schedule fires.

Context

What it's allowed to look at. A channel, a database, a folder, the public web.

Decision

The part that works something out — either rules you write down, or judgment you can only describe.

Action

What it does about it. Post, file, draft, update, flag, notify.

"If you can say — when X happens, go look at Y, work out Z, then do W — it's automatable. If you can't, it isn't yet, and the useful next step is figuring out which of the four is missing."

That last clause matters. Most ideas that feel impossible are missing exactly one block, usually the trigger. "I wish someone kept an eye on churn risk" has no trigger; "every Monday, check which accounts haven't been touched in 30 days" does. Coaching a squad from the first to the second is most of what you'll do during the brainstorm.

Teaching block · the second half of the idea

Two kinds of automation, and half of them don't think at all

The third block has two settings, and which one you pick decides what you're building. This is the credibility segment — saying plainly that much of what the team wants needs no model at all is what makes the agentic claims believable.

Scripts

The rule is something you can write down. Scan Jira for tickets sitting in "ready to deploy", work out who owns them, nudge them. No reasoning — just steps, run reliably, on time, forever.

Agents

You can't write the rule down, only describe what good looks like. Is this message a bug report? Is this news worth anyone's attention? Turn this thread into a decision record.

ScriptAgent
Decides byRules you can stateJudgment you can only describe
Same input twiceIdentical answer, alwaysUsually the same. Not guaranteed
Costs per runEffectively nothingReal, if small
When it's wrongIt breaks loudlyIt can be quietly plausible

"Can you write the rule down? It's a script. Can you only describe what good looks like? It needs judgment."

That last row of the table is the one to land hardest. A script that breaks tells you. An agent that's wrong can be quietly plausible — which is precisely why anything that has to be exact belongs on the left-hand side.

Most real automations are both, and the skill is carving the idea into the part you can write down and the small part you can't, then handing over only the second piece. It's the same instinct as the access rule: least information, least judgment. Every bit of either that you give away is a bit more that can drift.

Worked example — the BA-282 catch

Callback to the opening: this is the automation that caught BA-282. Walk the blocks and name the engine running each.

Ninety runs yesterday, eighty-nine of them silent, one worth someone's attention. Three of four blocks are plain steps, and judgment only gets involved after a script has thrown away everything obvious. Ask the room which blocks they'd have assumed needed AI.

Why a plain script still belongs in this session

Because the script was never the hard part. What stopped anyone building the Jira reconciler was getting Jira, GitHub and Slack to talk to each other, somewhere to keep the credentials, a machine to run it on at 9am every day, noticing when it silently stopped, and knowing Python.

All five are gone. You describe it and it writes it; it already holds the connections; it owns the schedule; it tells you when it breaks; and you change it by asking, in English. No server, no auth wiring, no Python. That's the empowerment message, and it's the reason the non-engineers in the room should care about the script half at all.

The three-question filter

For a first automation, all three need to be yes. Squads should apply this to their own ideas before pitching.

Frequency

Does it happen at least weekly? Automating something quarterly is a hobby project.

Judgment load

Is it annoying and low-judgment — the kind of thing you'd hand to a sharp new hire?

Error tolerance

For judgment-based ones: if it's 85% right, is it still useful? If it has to be exact every time, make it a script instead.

Where it's strong

  • Reading far more than a person has time to read
  • Doing the same careful thing every single time, without drift
  • Watching things nobody has been assigned to watch
  • Getting a first draft to 80% in seconds
  • Classifying and routing a messy inbox of anything

Where it isn't

  • Anything where being quietly wrong is expensive — pricing, contracts, legal, money out the door. That should be a script.
  • Work with no clear trigger, that happens "when it happens"
  • Judgment that depends on context nobody ever wrote down
  • Anything rare enough that you'd forget it exists
  • Being the last check before something reaches a customer
Teaching block · trust

What it can see, and what it can't

Our constraint isn't technical, it's trust — we keep access deliberately narrow, and that shapes what people should be designing. Say this plainly rather than letting the room infer it.

  • Access is granted by role, not by person's seniority. A role is a named list of tools. Today we run admin, operator, marketing and readonly roles, and most of the company has no role at all — which is why most of the company has never seen it work.
  • It doesn't roam. It works in the conversation it has been invited into, with the tools its role holds. It is not reading the company in the background.
  • Every squad gets a written scope card — exactly which channel, which database, which folder. Legible limits read as design; vague limits read as distrust.
  • Nothing customer-facing goes out unreviewed. Drafting is the job; sending stays with a person.

"The first design question for any automation is not what would I love to give it. It's what is the least information this could possibly need."

That reframe is the most transferable thing in the session. An automation that needs a trust decision waits on a conversation; an automation built on public data and one output channel ships this week. Tell squads to start outside the walls for their first build and earn their way inward.

Check before you say it. Confirm the exact wording on what the agent can and can't reach against the current config before the session — the specifics move, and this is the one segment where being loosely accurate is worse than saying less.
The demo

The Radar

Every weekday at 8am, the agent checks the outside world for news about our customers and prospects, and posts only what matters — with one line on why it matters to us.

Funding rounds. Exec changes at an account — the single best-known predictor of churn. Layoffs, acquisitions, product launches, a prospect announcing exactly the initiative we serve. Nobody at Base does this today, not because it's unimportant but because reading the news for forty companies every morning is nobody's job.

It needs almost no access, and that's the point. The public web, a list of company names, one channel. No customer data, no Notion workspace, no Slack history, no repo, no Drive. The list is the logos already published on our own homepage, so it discloses nothing that isn't already public — and it can live in the instruction itself rather than in a connected system. It is the strongest possible demonstration of the actual thesis: a deliberately tiny grant, producing outsized value.

Beat by beat

1

Show the cage first

Put the role definition on screen before anything runs. Three permissions. "This is everything it can touch. It cannot read our Notion, our Slack history, our code or our Drive. Watch what it can still do." Pre-empting the anxiety is what lets the room enjoy the next four minutes.

2

Ask for it in plain language, in Slack, in front of them

The fact that this paragraph is the entire authoring interface is a bigger mind-opener than the output. Slow down here and let people watch you type it.

Every weekday at 8am, look up recent news for each company on this list — funding, exec changes, layoffs, acquisitions, product launches, big hiring pushes. Post anything meaningful to #radar with a link and one line on why it matters to us. If there's nothing worth reporting, say nothing.
3

Run it once, immediately

Don't wait for tomorrow morning. Tell it to do today's pass now, and let the room watch the channel fill.

4

The payoff — turn to the person who owns it

Find the item about an account someone in the room owns, and ask them: "Did you know that?" After rehearsal you'll know which one to point at. Nothing else in the session competes with a room watching the agent tell a colleague something true about their own account that they didn't know.

5

Bridge straight into the squads

"Web, a list, a channel. Three permissions and one paragraph of English, and the company knows something it didn't know an hour ago. So — what's the version of that for your team?"

The one failure mode. A quiet news week produces a boring pass. Rehearsing the identical build the day before tells you in advance; if it's thin, widen the list with prospects and competitors, because something is always happening across forty companies. And if it genuinely is quiet, say so — "it reported nothing, because nothing happened. That's the design. It stays silent unless it matters." That's a good beat too.
Structure

Squads and champions

How to pick a champion

  • Curiosity over seniority. You want the person who tinkers, not the person who approves.
  • They own the squad's automation, its scope card, and being the person their team asks first.
  • They get a role in the config, a squad channel, and thirty minutes with you in the week after.
  • A champion who is too busy to build is the most common way this dies quietly.

Why squads are by function

  • Automations are function-specific — a mixed squad designs something nobody owns on Monday.
  • The champion becomes their function's go-to person afterwards, which is how this spreads without you.
  • Six squads of three to four: customer success, sales, marketing, support, product, R&D.

The artefact

Squad worksheet

One per squad, filled in by the end of the brainstorm. The access field is the important one — it turns scope into something squads design within and own, rather than something you veto afterwards.

In one line
When X happens, look at Y, work out Z, then do W.
Trigger
A message in a channel, a mention, or a schedule. Be specific about which.
Access needed
Exactly which channel, database, folder or public source. If this list is long, the idea is too big — shrink it.
What it works out
Classify, compare, summarise, decide, draft.
Rules or judgment
Which parts could you write down as rules, and which genuinely need judgment? Give the agent only the second kind.
What it does
The output, and where it lands.
Human checkpoint
What a person approves before anything leaves the building — or "none needed, it's internal".
Good enough looks like
If it's 85% right, what does that mean here, and is it still worth having?
Owner
The champion, plus who checks it's still useful in a month.
Afterwards

The demos, and what replaces a follow-up meeting

Three minutes per squad, at 2:38, with a visible timer. Six squads is seventeen minutes with nothing spare, so hold it. They run it live, however crude.

Say the bar out loud before they start, because it is deliberately low: it runs. Nobody has had an hour and also proved their automation helped anyone, and pretending otherwise makes the room defensive at exactly the wrong moment. Celebrate anything that executes. A squad that didn't get there shows its worksheet and names the blocker — that is a real result, and it hands you a list of things to unblock this week.

The honest version of the bar is that "it ran" is not "it helped" — but that test belongs to the Sunday report, not to a room an hour into building.

Without a follow-up meeting, something has to carry the loop, or a session like this decays inside a week. The good news is that it already exists and is already running: the automation-creation monitor announces every new automation the moment it appears, and the weekly activity report posts what everything did. Point both at a channel the whole company can see and the scoreboard becomes ambient instead of a meeting. Then close by having each champion say one sentence out loud about what they will finish this week.
